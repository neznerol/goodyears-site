How the numbers in Good Years are made

Good Years shows two figures: how long people who answered like you live on average, and how many of those years are typically spent in good health. This page describes every data source, every effect size and every modelling decision behind them, including the ones we are least sure of.

These are population estimates, not a prediction about you. They are averages across large groups of people, shown as ranges. Your own health and lifespan depend on many things no app can know, including genetics, medical history and chance. Good Years is for education and motivation only. It does not provide medical advice, diagnosis or treatment. For decisions about your health, talk to a doctor or qualified health professional.

1. In short

  • The starting point is the United Nations' life tables for your country, age and sex, projected forward using the UN's own assumptions about how mortality will improve.
  • The healthy share of life comes from World Health Organization data for your country and sex.
  • Each answer adjusts that starting point by an effect size taken from, or derived from, large long-term studies. The effects are scaled so that someone who answers like the average person gets their country's published figure.
  • Habits overlap, so their combined effect is held to what studies that measured several habits together actually found.
  • The model is checked against those studies automatically every time it changes, and a change that pushes it outside them is rejected.

2. The starting point: your country, age and sex

Life expectancy

The baseline is the United Nations World Population Prospects 2024 complete life tables: mortality by single year of age, 0 to 100+, for 237 countries and territories, for men and women separately. The most recent observed year is 2023.

A published life table holds today's death rates fixed for the rest of a life. That is the standard summary of current conditions, but a 40-year-old will live through the death rates of the coming decades, not today's. Using the fixed table understated life expectancy by roughly 4–5 years at age 40 and 6 years at 30. So Good Years projects each country's death rates forward using improvement rates fitted to the UN's own 2024–2070 projections, separately for every country, sex and age. The fit reproduces the UN's projected figures to within about a tenth of a year.

This is a moderately optimistic choice, and we say so. The UN's medium scenario assumes mortality at midlife improves by roughly 1.4–2.1% a year. Across the 2010s the UK and US actually saw 0.5–1.1%. Assuming no improvement at all would not have been neutral either, just less visible. The uncertainty this creates is carried in the range (section 5).

The healthy share of life

The WHO publishes healthy life expectancy (HALE): the years a population can expect to live in full health, with any loss of health weighted by how severe it is. That includes serious illness, but most of it is common, milder problems like back pain, low mood, hearing loss and eyesight.

Good Years does not use WHO's absolute HALE figures, because WHO's life expectancy and the UN's differ by several years for some countries. Instead it takes the healthy share of life (HALE divided by WHO life expectancy, both from WHO's 2021 data) and applies that share to the UN baseline. For example, the share is 0.878 for Denmark and 0.852 for the United States.

WHO publishes that share at birth. The share of remaining life that is healthy is lower at 60, and falls further with age. Good Years estimates the age-60 share from the birth share with one constant per sex (checked against WHO's own age-60 figures across all 185 countries, where it is within about 0.2 healthy years on average). After 60, the healthy share of remaining life declines by 0.4 percentage points a year. That rate was fitted to WHO's figures and the life tables, not chosen, and it sits in the same direction as, but less steep than, US survey data on disability-free years.

WHO publishes healthy-life figures for 185 of the 237 countries and territories.

3. How your answers adjust it

Hazard ratios

Long-term studies usually report a hazard ratio: how much higher or lower the death rate is, at any given age, for people in one group compared with a reference group. A hazard ratio of 1.30 means a 30% higher rate; 0.80 means 20% lower. Good Years multiplies the baseline death rate at every age from yours onwards by the hazard ratio for your answer, then recalculates life expectancy from the adjusted table. This is the standard way actuaries and epidemiologists turn a relative risk into years.

Measured against the average, not the ideal

Your country's life table already reflects a population that includes smokers, non-smokers, heavy drinkers and people who never drink. Applying hazard ratios measured against the best group would put almost everyone below their country's figure. So every answer's hazard ratio is divided by the population average for that question, weighted by how common each answer is. Someone who answers like the average person lands on their country's published figure. The shares of people giving each answer come from national surveys (NHANES, NHIS, BRFSS, Eurostat's EHIS and others). They describe how adults in high-income countries answer these questions, which is the right comparison for a questionnaire.

Habits overlap

People who smoke also tend to drink more; people who are inactive tend to weigh more. Multiplying each habit's full hazard ratio would count the same risk several times. Good Years adds up the (logarithmic) effects of your answers and then shrinks the total according to how many factors are really contributing. With one answer, nothing is shrunk, so a single factor reproduces its own study. With several, the combined effect is held near what Li et al. 2018 measured for five habits together. The strength of that shrinking was set by testing it against Li's figures, not by assumption.

This is why changes in the app can look smaller than the headlines you may have read. "Smokers lose about ten years" compares smokers with non-smokers who also differ in many other ways. Once you have told Good Years about those other ways, what it shows for quitting is the part that belongs to smoking alone.

Two further adjustments

  • The weight effect weakens with age. In the study behind the weight bands, the extra hazard per 5 units of BMI falls from 1.52 in people aged 35–49 to 1.21 at 70–89. Good Years applies the full effect up to 42, reduces it steadily to 45.5% of its (logarithmic) size by 80, and holds it there.
  • A one-question diet answer is a rough measure. The diet studies scored diet from detailed food questionnaires. One self-rated question agrees with that kind of score only weakly, and most people who misjudge their diet rate it better than it is. So the diet effect is reduced to 60% of the measured contrast. We intended to halve it, but at 50% the model no longer matched Li's combined figure, so the calibration set the floor.

4. Healthy years

Better answers are associated with more years of life and, in the research, with a larger share of those years in good health. Good Years moves your healthy share of remaining life up or down from your country's figure according to how your answers compare with the average, weighted by how much each factor affects mortality.

How far the share can move is the least certain number in the model, and we set it conservatively. The best-known study (Nyberg et al. 2020) counts years free of six major diagnosed conditions between ages 40 and 75, which makes the effect look larger than it would over a whole life. Measured to the end of life instead, Li et al. 2020 found a much smaller difference. HALE also counts every kind of ill health, much of which none of these questions touch. Taking both into account, the best and worst possible answers are allowed to differ by about 14 percentage points in healthy share.

5. Why it is a range

Every life figure is shown as a range. It combines two kinds of uncertainty:

  • What no questionnaire can know about anyone, sized at age 40 and scaled with remaining life: income and social position (about 1.8 years), family history (about 1.3), medical conditions the app does not ask about (about 1.7), how well study results carry between countries plus the uncertainty in the studies themselves (about 1.5), and whether mortality really improves as fast as the UN assumes (about 2.5). Combined, that is about ±4 years at 40. It never goes away, however many questions you answer.
  • What you have not told the app yet. For each question you skip, the range widens by how much life expectancy actually varies across the possible answers, weighted by how common each is. Skipping a question that matters a lot widens it a lot; skipping one that matters little barely moves it.

The range never starts below your current age.

6. Every question, and the evidence behind it

Each hazard ratio is marked with where it comes from:

  • published: the figure appears in the cited study;
  • derived: calculated from the study's reported results (for example by combining two published bands);
  • our choice: a modelling decision, explained in the note;
  • not yet checked: we have not been able to read the part of the paper the figure would come from.

7. How the model is checked

Every change to the model runs against a set of automated checks. The main ones hold it to studies that measured the result directly:

  • One factor on its own. Someone who answers only the smoking question must see a difference between smoking and never having smoked within 2.5 years of the ten Jha et al. found.
  • All habits together. The difference between the best and worst answers to everything must be within 2 years of what Li et al. 2018 (Circulation, 123,219 adults) measured at age 50 for five low-risk habits against none: 14.0 years for women, 12.2 for men.
  • Healthy years. The best-versus-worst difference in healthy years must be more than 9 years but less than 18. Nyberg et al. 2020 (JAMA Internal Medicine, 116,043 adults) found 9.9 years for men and 9.4 for women free of major chronic disease, counted only between 40 and 75, so a whole-life figure should be larger.
  • The average person. A profile with no answers must reproduce the country's published figure.
  • Every change the app offers must be associated with more healthy years and a larger healthy share of life. The automated check runs this for a profile with several unhealthy answers, in three countries and at four ages. A wider one-off sweep of 1,200 offered changes found the same.

When a proposed change to an effect size breaks one of these, the change is treated as too large, not the check as too strict.

8. Things the evidence made us change

Better answers are associated with more good years, not reliably with less time in poorer health

It would be simple, and appealing, to say that healthier habits shrink the years lived in poor health. The evidence doesn't support it. Living longer tends to add years at both ends. A review of 20 studies covering 26 cohorts (Dieteren et al. 2021) found that about half showed more years in poor health for people who never smoked than for smokers, while nearly all found a smaller share of life in poor health. Li et al. 2020 found the time in poor health got shorter for women and longer for men in the same study. In Good Years' own model, the gap between the two figures gets slightly wider for about half of 1,200 changes we tested.

What held for every change we tested is that a better answer is associated with more healthy years and a larger share of life in good health. That is what the app says, and it does not promise to close the gap.

Some answers are a reason to see a doctor, not a habit to change

Being underweight, sleeping more than nine hours, and not having had your blood pressure checked all carry a real statistical association. In each case the honest next step is to talk to a doctor, not to chase a number, so the app counts them in the estimate but never offers them as a change. The same goes for more serious hearing and eyesight difficulty.

"No significant difference" is not "the same"

An early version treated blood pressure under control as equivalent to normal, because the study's result for that group wasn't statistically significant. But not being able to show a difference is not showing there is none, and both studies we found put the figure above 1. It is now 1.15. Getting blood pressure under control is still associated with one of the largest differences the app shows.

9. What these numbers can't know

  • Your genetics and family history, which matter enormously and no questionnaire can capture.
  • Almost any medical condition you already live with. Diabetes is the one the app asks about; anything else — heart disease, cancer, lung or kidney conditions — is invisible to it, and the estimates will be least accurate for people living with one.
  • Where you live within your country, and your income, education, air quality, healthcare access and safety. The difference between the richest and poorest in one country can exceed anything on this page.
  • The extremes. The outermost answers sit at the edge of what the studies measured. Someone sleeping two hours a night is worse off than the "under 5 hours" answer suggests.
  • Most of the research comes from Europe and North America. Good Years assumes the relative effects carry across countries, which is standard practice and also the model's largest simplification.
  • Men and women get the same hazard ratios; only their life tables differ. Li et al. found a larger best-versus-worst difference for women, and the model gives a slightly larger one for men. Fixing it would mean inventing sex-specific figures for most factors, which the sources don't provide.
  • How common each answer is doesn't change with age in the model, although in reality it does, most for diabetes and blood pressure.
  • Healthy life expectancy is a population measure built from severity weights. It describes a population's typical experience, not anyone's diagnosis.

10. What we left out, and why

Many things are associated with longer life in the research. A factor gets a question only if a large study supports it, the question can be answered honestly without a device or a test, and it doesn't just repeat something already asked. Some that didn't make it:

  • Sitting time. In a harmonised analysis of 1,005,791 people (Ekelund et al. 2016), sitting more than 8 hours a day showed no clear effect in the most active quarter. It would mostly count inactivity twice.
  • Daily step count. A real dose-response, but it measures the same thing as the exercise question, and nobody can report their steps without a device.
  • Water intake. No association in the pooled evidence.
  • Marriage or partnership. A sizeable association, but nothing anyone can honestly be advised to change.
  • Purpose in life. The association mostly appears in the years just before death, which suggests illness lowers the sense of purpose rather than the other way round.
  • Sauna use. One cohort, one country, men only, and an effect that fades with longer follow-up.
  • Social contact, tooth loss and road safety were in an earlier version and removed. Social contact looks more like a marker than a cause in the stronger evidence. Tooth loss largely repeats smoking, diet and income. Road safety changed nobody's figure except motorcyclists and people who sometimes skip a seatbelt. Together, the three narrowed the range by days.
